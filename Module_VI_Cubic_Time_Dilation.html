VI Cubic time dilation

The Lorentz factor, taken to the cube root

The GNSS proposal replaced the traditional exponent −1/2 with −1/3, originally claimed on the strength of 1,000 simulations against live constellation data — a claim excluded by independent measurement elsewhere in this project (status below). At satellite speeds the models differ by parts in 1011 — exactly the regime where nanoseconds become meters of position error. Push the slider relativistic and watch the two models tear apart.

Status · excluded as a clock law, kinematically
A storage-ring measurement (Botermann et al. 2014, Li⁺ at β = 0.338) already bounds |k−½| < 1.9×10−8; an independent fit across 14 one-electron ions gives k̂ = 0.499962 ± 0.000102, excluding k = ⅓ at 1626σ (Cubic Time Dimensions in Quantum Mechanics, 2nd ed. revised, §7.1). Paired with Module XI's own gravitational exclusion at 4762σ, the cube-root exponent does not survive as a literal clock rate in either channel. The simulations below remain useful as a demonstration of how far two candidate rate laws would diverge at satellite speed — but the (1−β²)−1/3 exponent itself now recurs only where the Engine's own cross-check places it: strong-field radiated power (Airy structure), not a clock rate.

The array is every operational GNSS satellite, 145 birds across six constellations, with the eccentricity clock correction as the sharpest non-arbitrary discriminator between k = ½ and k = ⅓ (17% amplitude split). The live almanac fetch is the most fragile part and the least important: vis-viva makes β² and Φ affinely dependent at fixed semi-major axis, so rotating every satellite's phase through a full turn moves k̂ by at most 1.8×10−7 — live data would not improve the measurement. The near-degeneracy between β² and Φ (r = 0.968) is broken by adding ground stations, confirmed by both a correlation and an independent error-ratio check.
